Overview

This homework builds the core operations of a geometric modeler such as evaluating Bézier curves and patches, shading triangle meshes with vertex normals, editing half-edge connectivity, and refining meshes with Loop subdivision.

The most interesting lesson was that a surface depends on both its positions and its connectivity. Vertex normals can make a coarse mesh look smooth without changing its geometry, while subdivision actually changes that geometry. A symmetric shape can refine asymmetrically when its triangulation gives equivalent corners different neighborhoods.

01

Bézier Curves with
1D de Casteljau Subdivision

Repeated linear interpolation

De Casteljau’s algorithm evaluates a Bézier curve at a parameter t between 0 and 1. At each level, it interpolates between adjacent points, producing one fewer point than the previous level. Repeating this process until one point remains gives the curve’s value at t.

One evaluation level
pi(k+1) = (1 − t) pi(k) + t pi+1(k)

In BezierCurve::evaluateStep(), I iterate over neighboring pairs, compute their interpolation, and append each result to a new vector. The function returns that next vector and performs exactly one level. Keeping each level separate allows the viewer to display the construction step by step.

Evaluation sequence

White squares are the original controls, blue squares are intermediate points, and the final red square lies on the green curve.

Moving the controls and changing t

I moved the original controls into a double-dip shape and changed t. Moving controls changes the curve itself. Changing t moves the evaluated point along that curve. The evaluation point move toward the first endpoint as t approaches 0, and toward the last endpoint as it approaches 1.

In these modified-curve captures, the red point moves from the central crest toward the right-hand dip as the evaluation parameter changes.

02

Bézier Surfaces with
Separable 1D de Casteljau

From a curve to a patch

For a grid of 3D control points, I first treat each row as a Bézier curve and evaluate it at u. These row results become the controls for another curve, which I evaluate at v. The final result is the point on the surface at (u, v).

I reused the same interpolation rule in BezierPatch::evaluateStep(), now with 3D vectors and an explicit parameter. evaluate1D() repeatedly applies that step until one point remains. Finally, evaluate() calls the 1D evaluator on every row at u, collects those results, and evaluates that collection at v.

03

Area-Weighted
Vertex Normals

Traversing the one-ring

Starting from a half-edge rooted at the vertex, I visit each incident face by advancing through twin() followed by next(), stopping when I return to the starting half-edge. For each real triangular face, I take its three positions and compute the cross product of two edge vectors.

Area-weighted normal
nv = normalize( Σf incident to v (p1 − p0) × (p2 − p0) )

The cross product’s magnitude is twice the triangle area, so summing the unnormalized cross products automatically weights larger faces more heavily. I normalize only after summing all contributions.

Flat shading versus Phong shading

Flat shading makes individual triangles visible through abrupt changes in lighting. With area-weighted vertex normals, Phong shading interpolates the normals across triangles and produces a gradual lighting transition across the body, spout, and handle.

The wireframe and silhouette remain those of the same mesh. The visible smoothing comes from the normals used for shading.

04

Edge Flip

Reconnecting two triangles

For an interior edge shared by two triangles, I gather the six local half-edges, four vertices, five edges, and two faces before making any changes. The shared edge is redirected to connect the two opposite vertices. I then use setNeighbors() to assign the next, twin, vertex, edge, and face references for each affected half-edge.

I also update the representative half-edge references of the affected vertices, edges, and faces. Boundary edges return unchanged. A flip touches a fixed local neighborhood, takes O(1) work, and creates or deletes no elements.

Keeping the pointers consistent

The important debugging step was labeling all six half-edges and planning their connectivity before updating pointers. Updating the triangle cycles alone was insufficient. The representative vertex half-edges also had to remain rooted at the correct vertices after the diagonal changed. I used complete setNeighbors() assignments and tested consecutive flips to check that later edits could still traverse the mesh.

The changed diagonal is visible near the center of the teapot’s body. Vertex positions and element counts stay fixed, but on a nonplanar patch, changing the diagonal can change the local surface and its shading.

05

Edge Split

Building the four new triangle cycles

For an interior edge, I gather the original local neighborhood and insert a vertex at the midpoint of the edge’s endpoints. I allocate one new vertex, three new edges, two new faces, and six new half-edges, reusing the existing elements for the rest of the configuration.

Split position
pm = ½ (pb + pc)

The midpoint connects to the two opposite vertices, replacing the two original triangles with four. I assign every affected half-edge’s neighbors and refresh the representative references for the vertices, edges, and faces. Boundary edges are left unchanged in this implementation. As with a flip, the amount of local work is O(1).

Debugging the larger neighborhood

The extra elements made splitting more demanding than flipping. I labeled both the original and new half-edges, then grouped them by the four resulting triangles so that each triangle had an explicit closed cycle. I also made the inserted vertex’s representative half-edge point along a half of the original edge, rather than along a new spoke to an opposite vertex.

For subdivision, I distinguish the two halves of the original edge from the newly introduced connecting edges using isNew flags. I tested repeated splits and combinations of splits and flips, since an incomplete pointer update may become visible only when a later operation revisits the neighborhood.

Splits alone, then splits with flips

The left capture is the baseline for both comparisons. Splitting adds smaller triangles around the edited region while preserving the existing piecewise planar surface. Combining splits and flips adds resolution and changes the local diagonal arrangement. In the combined capture, this also produces visible changes in the central triangles’ shading.

06

Loop Subdivision
for Mesh Upsampling

Compute, refine, then commit positions

In MeshResampler::upsample(), I combine the split and flip operations with weighted position updates. I first compute the next positions of the original vertices and the vertices that will be inserted along the original edges. Those values are stored in Vertex::newPosition and Edge::newPosition, while the current mesh positions remain unchanged.

Interior edge rule
pedge = ⅜ (A + B) + ⅛ (C + D)

Here, A and B are the edge endpoints, and C and D are the opposite vertices in its two incident triangles. An original vertex moves according to its degree n and the positions of its original neighbors:

Original vertex rule
p′ = (1 − nβ) p + β Σneighbors pjβ = 3/16 for n = 3; otherwise β = 3/(8n).
  1. Cache the coarse-mesh positions.Compute every weighted update from the original mesh, before changing connectivity or positions.
  2. Split every original edge once.I save the original edges in a separate vector so newly created edges are not split again during the same pass. Each inserted vertex receives its edge’s cached position.
  3. Flip the appropriate new edges.Flip new connecting edges with one old and one new endpoint. The two halves of an original edge are retained.
  4. Apply the cached positions.After connectivity is complete, assign the stored positions to all vertices. Each original triangle has become four triangles.

Saving the original edge list and maintaining the isNew flags were the key bookkeeping details. Updating positions in place too early would mix old and new geometry in later weighted averages, making the result depend on traversal order.

What happens to sharp features?

The torus sequences show the angular ring becoming progressively rounder as its triangles become denser. The outer corners and the corners around the hole soften because the position updates average each vertex with its neighbors. The geometry also contracts toward a smoother limit surface.

I pre-split the torus’s outer-ring edges to retain more of its hexagonal outline. Those added vertices change the neighborhoods used by the weighted averages. Compared with the unsplit ring, the pre-split sequence retains a clearer six-sided tendency, while both versions become smoother. The splits reduce the rounding of the outer silhouette, but standard Loop subdivision still smooths the surface rather than preserving a perfectly sharp crease.

Why does a symmetric cube become asymmetric?

A cube’s positions are symmetric, but a diagonal on each square face selects one pair of opposite corners. Across the cube, those choices can give geometrically equivalent corners different vertex degrees or different neighbor arrangements. Loop subdivision uses both, so those corners need not receive equivalent weighted updates.

In the unprocessed sequence, the first refinement already has a visibly uneven outline in this face-on view. Further refinements smooth the surface, but retain the bias inherited from the original triangulation. Increasing the resolution alone does not remove that difference.

Making the starting triangulation symmetric

I split the diagonal on all six faces of the cube. Each split inserts a face-center vertex and replaces the two face triangles with four. This removes the privileged face-diagonal direction. Each original cube corner is then connected to its three neighboring corners and the centers of its three adjacent faces, giving all eight corners degree six with equivalent neighborhoods.

This symmetric connectivity makes equivalent corners receive symmetric position updates. The pre-split screenshots show a more balanced rounded-square outline through the refinement sequence. Corners still soften because the Loop rules remain smoothing rules; preprocessing addresses the triangulation bias rather than locking the cube’s sharp edges.
